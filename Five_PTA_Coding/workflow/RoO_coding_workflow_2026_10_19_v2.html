Coding Workflow for RoO Restricted-Input Pairs

General coding rules for every preferential trade agreement (PTA). The definition of an input–output link is expansive: every pair starts at DV = 1, and each step turns to 0 only the cases it clearly excludes. Pairs are written input → output (A → B). The rules include the scope decisions of 19 October 2026; they are summarised in the last section. Results for individual PTAs are reported separately.

Step 1. Take in the RoO pairs and set DV = 1

  • Read each agreement’s product-specific rules and list every (restricted input, output) pair at HS6, in the agreement’s own HS vintage.
  • Map every pair to a common HS vintage with the WCO/UN correlation tables. A code that splits produces one pair per new code; flag non-one-to-one mappings as concord_flag = 1 for later checking.
  • Remove duplicate pairs within each agreement and keep a pair-level master list across agreements (used in step 7).
  • Set DV = 1 for every pair. Steps 3–6 can only turn a 1 into a 0; no step turns a 0 back into a 1.

Step 2. Lock identical or same-good pairs at 1

Rule: set locked = 1 (route = same_good) when input and output are the same good or very similar: the same HS6 code, or the same species, material or variety differing only in processing stage, preservation state, packing or size of presentation, or a treatment the producer can add (roasting, decaffeinating, dyeing, printing, plying). Locked pairs skip steps 3–6.

Boundary with siblings (step 4). “Very similar” means one can be turned into the other. Goods that differ in a fixed attribute — fibre, species, gender cut, base material — are siblings, not the same good: women’s cotton jackets → women’s synthetic jackets is not locked and goes to 0 at step 4. The direction must also be possible: dyed fabric → unbleached fabric is not locked and goes to 0 at step 5.

Step 3. Turn to 0: clearly irrelevant pairs

Rule: turn to 0 only when no route from step 6 could apply: the input would have to change into a different species, plant, fibre or substance, and it is not a constituent, feed, part or waste of the output. If in doubt, leave the pair at 1 for step 6.

Caution. Do not turn a pair to 0 at this step when the output is a mixture, a farmed animal or a waste product; leave it at 1 for step 6. Example: chicken cuts → 160249 (prepared pork and mixtures) looks irrelevant but is a mixture pair.

Fibres. Different synthetic fibres — nylon, polyester, acrylic, polypropylene — count as one fibre: nylon yarn → acrylic yarn is not a step-3 pair; it is an intermediate sibling (step 4) and stays 1. Different natural or artificial fibres (cotton → polyester, flax → jute, viscose → polyester) remain different fibres.

Fish and other seafood. Different species are different goods: the pair is 0 at this step unless the input could feed the output (step 6, feed). The intermediate-sibling rule of step 4 does not apply to different species.

Step 6. Turn to 0 only where the expansive definition finds no route

Rule: every pair still at 1 is checked against the expansive routes below. It stays 1 if any route plausibly applies (record the route). It is turned to 0 only if you are sure no route exists — the cases listed in each table.

General principle. Use the broadest scope: keep 1 wherever any production route is technically possible, even if it is uncommon. If in doubt, keep 1.

Minor constituents of textile goods (route = ingredient or mixture)

A garment, made-up article, yarn or fabric is classified by its main fibre, but it can contain other fibres: lining, pocketing, trim or blend. A fibre, yarn, fabric or waste of another fibre going into it stays 1:

  • certain 1 when the input is cotton or a man-made fibre (synthetic or artificial). These are the usual blend partners and lining materials, and the HS classifies a mixed good by the fibre that predominates by weight, so a “cotton” garment can contain almost half man-made fibre;
  • 1 with uncertain = 1 when the input is another fibre (wool, silk, flax, ramie, coarse animal hair, other textile materials), and when fabric of the same fibre but another construction goes into a garment (woven fabric → knitted garment and the reverse). These pairs count in DV but not in DV_strict.

Feed (route = feed)

The input must be a feed-grade good — whole low-value fish (herring, sardines, mackerel, other fish), fish meat and meal, crustaceans and their meal (krill), molluscs — and the output a farmed carnivorous fish or crustacean (trout, salmon, eels, sea bass, flatfish, bluefin tuna, shrimps and prawns, crabs) or a product made from it. Wild-caught outputs (other tunas, skipjack, cod, herring, plaice) and grazers or filter feeders are 0. Where a feed use is possible but not established, the pair is 1 with uncertain = 1.

Two AI coders and conflicts

  • Setup. Two independent coders, ideally from different model families, receive the same codebook (steps 2–6 with these examples) and the same pair. Neither sees the other’s answer.
  • Units. To code tens of thousands of pairs, pairs are grouped by heading pair (input HS4 × output HS4). Each coder gives one default code per unit plus exceptions for single input codes, output codes or pairs, with the 5 most similar coded pairs as worked examples.
  • Question asked. Each coder decides whether the pair should be turned from 1 to 0, and at which step, and returns {DV, route, step, one-sentence reason}.
  • Combining rule. Both say 0: DV = 0. Both say 1: DV = 1. If they disagree, the pair stays 1 with uncertain = 1, and both codes and reasons are kept.
  • Decided by rule. Where a scope convention (last section) settles a disagreement — for example intermediate siblings, chemicals → wastes, minerals → cement — the pair is coded by that convention with uncertain = 0.
  • Strict robustness DV. Count only certain 1s: DV_strict = DV × (1 − uncertain), optionally restricted to forward, ingredient and component routes.
  • Reliability. Report Cohen’s kappa between the two coders. Then code a stratified sample of about 300 pairs across sources, steps and routes to estimate the accuracy of the final codes, weighting each stratum by its size. Without a domain expert, the sample is coded by two new AI sessions that see only the codebook and the pairs (no earlier answer), and a person reviews every pair where they disagree with the final code. Errors that form a group are corrected by rule in every PTA, not pair by pair. Report it as an independent AI audit, not as expert hand-coding.

Step 7. Scale up

(a) Exact matches

Any pair already in the coded master list (same HS6 pair after concordance) takes the existing DV, route and uncertain values, with source = exact_copy. Exact copies are re-copied whenever the master labels change, so a pair has the same code in every PTA.

(b) Train a model on the coded pairs

  • Features: input and output chapters and headings; same-chapter and same-heading flags; flags from the step 2, 4 and 5 rules; text embeddings of both descriptions and their interaction.
  • Target: whether the pair stays 1 or is turned to 0 (optionally also route and step).
  • Validation: hold out whole chapter pairs (grouped cross-validation) so the model cannot memorise near-duplicate pairs; random cross-validation overstates accuracy.

(c) Apply with confidence bands

Predicted probabilityAction
≥ 0.9Keep at 1 (source = ml)
≤ 0.1Turn to 0 (source = ml)
Between 0.1 and 0.9Send through steps 2–6 with the two AI coders, giving them the 5 most similar coded pairs as worked examples

Support condition. Use the bands only where the chapter pair has more than 30 coded pairs in the master list, and only for pair types where the audit (d) shows the bands are reliable. Other pairs go to the AI coders or to explicit rules.

(d) Audit

Check a random sample of model-coded pairs from each band against the two AI coders (or a person). If accuracy in a band falls below the threshold, narrow that band and send more pairs to the AI coders. When the model and an explicit rule disagree, the rule is applied where it finds a route; otherwise the pair goes to the AI coders.

(e) Explicit rules for structured chapters

Textile chapters 50–63 are coded by explicit rules. Each code has a stage (fibre, yarn, fabric, trim, made-up or garment, waste), a fibre and, for fabrics and garments, a construction (knitted or not). The rules apply steps 2–6 and the conventions above: forward stage with the same fibre = 1; minor constituents as in step 6; finished siblings = 0; backward = 0; intermediate siblings of one fibre = 1; sewing thread and trims → articles = component; waste reprocessed = 1.

Reading “of other textile materials”. In the HS this means every fibre that is not named in the same group of subheadings. 610990 T-shirts “of other textile materials” include synthetic T-shirts, because only cotton has its own subheading (610910), so 540231 nylon yarn → 610990 is a main-fibre (forward) pair. 620449 dresses of other textile materials exclude wool, cotton, synthetic and artificial fibres, which have their own subheadings.

Example. Another agreement restricts 030342 frozen yellowfin tuna → 160414 prepared tuna; the exact pair is already coded, so it is copied and stays 1 (forward). A new pair, 030389 other frozen fish → 160420 other prepared fish, has close neighbours coded 1 (forward, feed); the model gives p = 0.94 and it stays 1. A third pair, 230120 fishmeal → 020714 chicken cuts, falls in the middle band; the AI coders disagree (fishmeal as poultry feed), so it stays DV = 1 with uncertain = 1.
